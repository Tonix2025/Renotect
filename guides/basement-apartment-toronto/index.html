How to build a legal basement apartment in Toronto

Short answer: yes, in Toronto you can add a basement apartment to a detached house, a semi-detached house or a townhouse. The City calls it a secondary suite. To make it legal you need a building permit, which means drawings that show the suite meets the Ontario Building Code for ceiling height, exits, fire safety and alarms. Most basements can be made to work; the question is how much has to change.

Here is what the rules cover, what usually decides the budget, and how the permit process works.

Which houses can have a basement apartment?

The City of Toronto permits a secondary suite in an existing detached house, semi-detached house or townhouse. The suite must be smaller in area than the main unit. A basement apartment is the most common type, but a secondary suite can also be on another floor of the house.

If you are thinking about more than two units, for example a basement apartment plus a garden or laneway suite, or converting the house into a multiplex, the rules and the drawings are different, so it is worth checking the options before you start.

The Building Code basics

A secondary suite is a separate home, so the Code treats it differently from a finished basement used by the same family. The main points the drawings have to cover are:

  • Ceiling height. The 2024 Ontario Building Code allows a minimum ceiling height of 1.95 m in a secondary suite, with 1.85 m clear under beams and ducts. Many older basements are lower than this, and that is often the biggest decision: lowering the floor (underpinning or a bench footing) or rerouting ducts.
  • Exits. The suite needs a safe way out, usually its own entrance. Depending on the layout, a separate side or rear entrance with an exterior stair may be needed, and a new basement walk-out has its own permit details.
  • Bedroom windows. In a house without sprinklers, each bedroom normally needs a window big enough to escape through, or a door to the outside. A small basement window often has to be enlarged, with a window well outside.
  • Fire safety between units. The two units have to be separated, by a fire separation or, where the Code allows it in a house with a secondary suite, by a continuous smoke-tight barrier. Ducts, doors and openings between the units need care.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms. Smoke alarms in both units must be interconnected, so an alarm in the basement also sounds upstairs, and carbon monoxide alarms are needed where there are fuel-burning appliances. Their locations are shown on the permit drawings.
  • Heating, ventilation, plumbing and electrical. The suite needs its own kitchen and bathroom. Heating and ventilation have to be designed so smoke cannot travel easily between units, and electrical work is inspected separately by the Electrical Safety Authority.

What decides the budget

Every basement is different, so we do not quote a price per square foot. The items that move the budget the most are:

  • Whether the ceiling is high enough or the floor has to be lowered
  • Whether a new entrance has to be cut into the foundation
  • How many windows have to be enlarged
  • Where the existing plumbing stack and drains are, and whether the new kitchen and bathroom can be placed near them
  • The condition of the existing wiring, furnace and ductwork

A site visit and measured drawings answer these questions early, before money is spent on finishes.

The permit process, step by step

  • Site visit and measurements. The existing basement is measured, including ceiling heights, windows, the stair and the mechanical room.
  • Layout and design. The suite is laid out around the Code requirements: entrance, bedrooms with egress windows, kitchen and bathroom near the drains.
  • Permit drawings. The City asks for a site plan, floor plans with smoke and carbon monoxide alarm locations, sections with ceiling heights, and construction details. The drawings must be prepared by a qualified designer, who signs them with their BCIN, or by an architect or engineer.
  • Application and review. The application is submitted online through Toronto Building. The City checks it against the Building Code and zoning and may ask for revisions.
  • Construction and inspections. Building inspectors visit at set stages of the work, and electrical work has its own inspection.

If the work includes lowering the floor, a new basement entrance or new beams, a structural engineer usually designs those parts, and the designer coordinates them into the permit set.

What about an existing basement apartment?

Many Toronto houses already have a basement apartment that was built without a permit. It can usually be made legal, but it has to meet the same requirements as a new suite, so it often needs upgrades such as interconnected alarms, a fire separation or a larger bedroom window. The first step is the same: measure what is there and compare it with the Code.

The bottom line

A legal basement apartment is achievable in most Toronto detached, semi-detached and row houses. The ceiling height, the entrance and the bedroom windows usually decide how much work it takes, so check them first. Then a set of permit drawings that answers the Building Code questions clearly makes the City review smoother.

FAQ

Common questions

Do I need a permit for a basement apartment in Toronto?

Yes. Adding a secondary suite to a house needs a building permit from the City of Toronto, with drawings that show the suite meets the Ontario Building Code.

What is the minimum ceiling height for a basement apartment?

Under the 2024 Ontario Building Code, a secondary suite can have a minimum ceiling height of 1.95 m, with 1.85 m clear under beams and ducts. If the basement is lower, the floor may need to be lowered or ducts rerouted.

Does a basement apartment need a separate entrance?

The suite needs a safe exit that meets the Code. In most houses this means a separate entrance, at the side or rear or through a new basement walk-out, and the exact solution depends on the layout.

Can an existing illegal basement apartment be made legal?

Usually yes. It has to meet the same requirements as a new suite, so it often needs upgrades such as interconnected smoke alarms, a fire separation or larger bedroom windows, and a building permit.

Who can prepare the drawings for a basement apartment?

A designer qualified in the House category with a BCIN, an architect or an engineer. The drawings carry the designer's name and BCIN.
